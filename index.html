Conventions used in this trainer

1 · Sides

The market maker quotes two prices. The left-hand side is the maker's bid — the maker buys the base currency. The right-hand side is the maker's offer — the maker sells the base currency.

Client saysSideClient doesMaker does
"Yours" / "I give"LHSsells basebuys base
"Mine" / "I pay"RHSbuys basesells base

Perspective changes direction only, never the price. Both counterparties deal at the same level; the maker earns the spread against mid, the taker pays it.

2 · Naming a swap

An FX swap is named by what you do with the base currency, near leg first. "Buy and sell EUR/USD" = buy EUR spot, sell EUR forward.

SwapFar legFundingPoints
Buy / sellsell baseborrow base, lend quoteSHORT
Sell / buybuy baselend base, borrow quoteLONG

The one shortcut that never fails: ignore the near leg. Ask only "which way am I in the base currency on the far leg?" Buy far → long points. Sell far → short points.

3 · Sides of a swap price

The side of a swap quote is defined by the far leg, in the base currency — exactly mirroring spot.

Sign trap: the RHS is always the numerically higher number, even when points are negative (−204.7 / −200.7). Many screens display negative points descending and without signs, which is a well-known way to deal the wrong side.

4 · Points and rates

points ≈ S × (rquote − rbase) × t

Points are positive when the quote currency has the higher rate, negative when the base currency does. So "USD rates up" is not one answer: in EUR/USD, USD is the quote → points up. In USD/JPY, USD is the base → points down. Always identify base vs quote before answering anything.

5 · Cross-currency basis

The basis is quoted on the non-USD leg and is usually negative — that negative number is the premium paid to obtain USD through the FX swap market.

BasisEffectEUR/USD ptsUSD/JPY pts
more negativeUSD scarcehigherlower
less negativeUSD easylowerhigher

The asymmetry is only about where USD sits in the quote. Derive it, don't memorise it: a more negative basis lowers the effective non-USD rate implied by the swap, then apply the points formula.

For USD-base pairs the future runs opposite to the spot quote. Getting that backwards doubles your risk instead of hedging it. A futures hedge also leaves date-mismatch (points/basis) risk between the IMM date and your actual cash flow.

7 · Event chain

Run it in three separate steps, every time:

1. Which currency's rates move, and which way?
2. Is that currency the base or the quote? → points direction via the formula.
3. Separately: rates up ⇒ currency stronger ⇒ pair up or down depending on base/quote.

Spot and points do not move together in any fixed way. And a genuine risk-off shock adds a second-order basis effect that can push points against the rate-differential effect.

Pips ↔ bp. For 4-decimal pairs the pip is 0.0001, so the bracket is 1 and pips per bp = spot × tenor. EUR/USD at 1.10, 3M → 0.275 pips per bp, i.e. 1 pip ≈ 3.6bp. For JPY pairs the pip is 0.01, so divide by 100: USD/JPY at 150, 3M → 0.375 pips per bp.

Pip in cash. For any XXX/USD pair, 1 pip on 1mio = $100 — so 100mio is $10,000 a pip, flat. For USD/XXX pairs the pip pays in the quote currency, so divide by spot: 100mio USD/JPY at 150 → 1,000,000 JPY ÷ 150 ≈ $6,667, and that value moves with spot.

9 · Cross-currency basis — pay vs receive

The spread sits on the non-USD leg. Follow the principal: you receive interest on what you lent.

Side → basis, instantly. A basis quote is just another two-way, and the RHS is the numerically higher number (−16 is the RHS against −20). The taker always gets the worse side:

Taker dealsDoesFundingWants basis
RHSPAYS the basislend USDless negative
LHSRECEIVES the basisborrow USDmore negative

If you are the maker you are on the opposite side of that table. RHS = pay = buy = long — the same rule as spot and as swap points. One rule, three products.

The identity to internalise: receive the basis ≡ borrow USD ≡ sell/buy EUR/USD ≡ long the forward points. Four names for one trade. If you can move between them without pausing, the whole product clicks.

P&L = move in bp × DV01, and the sign check is always the same: you are locked at your level, so re-do the trade at the new level and net the two.

10 · Carry

Carry is what happens to your P&L if spot never moves and the curve never changes. Points converge to zero at maturity, and that convergence is the carry.

Buying the high-yielder forward earns carry. Buying the low-yielder forward pays it. The high-yielder always trades at a forward discount — you buy below spot and the discount rolls up in your favour.

The yen carry trade. USD rates well above JPY rates ⇒ USD/JPY points deeply negative. Buy USD/JPY 12M forward maybe 400 pips below spot; hold; if spot is unchanged you collect 400 pips. Economically you borrowed cheap JPY and held USD.

Why it exists. Covered interest parity says the price is fair — you are not being handed free money, you are being paid to take spot risk. Uncovered interest parity predicts JPY should appreciate by exactly the differential and wipe out the gain; empirically it usually doesn't. That gap is the forward premium puzzle, and it is the carry trade's entire edge.

What you are actually short: crash risk. Carry bleeds slowly and loses violently — in risk-off everyone unwinds at once, JPY spikes, and months of carry go in days.

11 · Forward point math

F = S × (1 + rquote·t) ÷ (1 + rbase·t)
points = (F − S) ÷ pip
rquote implied = [ (F/S)(1 + rbase·t) − 1 ] ÷ t

Approximation good enough to quote from: points ≈ S × (rq − rb) × t ÷ pip. Sign-check before you calculate — if the arithmetic disagrees with the sign, the arithmetic is wrong.

Day count matters: t = days/360 for most, ACT/365 for GBP and AUD. Wrong basis ≈ 1.4% error on the points.

The implied-yield inversion is the important one. Back out the USD rate the swap market is charging, compare it to cash USD rates: implied above cash ⇒ dollars expensive via swaps ⇒ negative basis. That gap IS the basis.

12 · The hidden spot component

A swap with the same notional on both legs cancels nominally — but not in present value. The near leg settles now, undiscounted; the far leg is discounted.

net base PV = N × (1 − DF) ≈ N × rbase × T

100mio EUR 1Y buy/sell at 2% EUR leaves you long ≈2mio EUR of genuine spot delta, even though the position sheet reads flat. One big figure on spot is ≈$20,000. Desks either hedge it in spot or adjust the far-leg notional away.

Seen from the other side: the quote legs differ by exactly the points, the base legs differ by exactly the discounting. Those two mismatches are what an FX swap is.

13 · Convexity bias

A future pays its P&L in cash today; an FRA pays at the end, discounted. When the thing moving is the interest rate, that timing is worth money.

Short the future (positioned for higher rates): rates rise → margin arrives now → reinvest at the new higher rate. Rates fall → you pay margin now → but fund it at the new lower rate. The timing always favours the short.

Nobody takes the long side unpaid, so futures rate > FRA rate. The gap is the convexity bias — it grows with the square of maturity and with volatility. Trivial in the whites, material in the reds and greens. Never paste futures rates into a curve unadjusted.

14 · Central bank liquidity — the Scandi read

The Riksbank drains SEK weekly by auctioning certificates. Banks bid to park surplus SEK.

AuctionSEK liquiditySEK basis
Weak / undersubscribedstays in the systemmore negative (left)
Strong / fully takendrained outless negative (right)

Generalise, don't memorise: abundant local currency ⇒ local currency cheap to borrow ⇒ non-USD leg spread more negative. The same read applies to Norges Bank's daily NOK purchases, ECB liquidity repayments, or any operation that adds or drains local cash.

Scandi basis markets are small and flow-driven — covered-bond issuers swapping foreign issuance home, plus the central bank. Flow beats fundamentals here.

15 · Mark to market

MTM = N × (F1 − F0) × DFquote
F1 − F0 = (S1 − S0) + (pts1 − pts0)

Mark to the date, not the tenor. A 6M forward dealt 4 months ago is revalued against today's 2M forward to that same value date. Comparing against today's 6M imports curve slope that has nothing to do with your trade.

Attribution. Split every mark into a spot component and a points component — they belong to different books. Spot usually dominates by an order of magnitude, which is precisely why an unhedged outright is a bad way to express a points view.

A swap's mark is pure points. The two legs carry the same base notional in opposite directions, so spot nets out. Points move × pip value × direction, and remember buy/sell is short the points. (The small residual is the PV mismatch from §12.)

Discounting. PV each currency's flow on its own OIS curve, then convert at today's spot. The N × ΔF × DF shortcut is exact only while the legs are matched. Roughly 1% of the mark at 3M, ~8% at 2Y.

Historic rate rollover. Rolling a losing forward at the original rate rather than market spot does not remove the loss — it lends it to the client. Price the funding into the new points, book the credit exposure, get it signed off. It is policed because it hides losses.

Sign first, size second. Almost every marking error is a sign error, not an arithmetic one.

16 · Shorthand — and the inversion trap

On the desk nobody says the pair. "I pay SEK", "s/b AUD", "give me EUR 3M". You have to resolve it yourself, and two kinds of shorthand behave differently.

SaidPairBase
EUR, GBP, AUD, NZDEUR/USD etc.that currency
JPY, CHF, CAD, SEK, NOKUSD/JPY etc.USD

Naming a SIDE never inverts. "Pay" / "give" always refers to the pair's points, whichever currency was spoken. Pay = RHS = buy the base forward = long the points. Always.

Naming a SWAP does invert when the spoken currency is the quote. "b/s SEK" = buy SEK near = sell USD near = sell/buy USD/SEK = long the points. Exactly backwards from "b/s USD/SEK".

Worked example: b/s SEK → borrowed SEK, lent USD → long USD/SEK points → want SEK rates up. Hot Swedish CPI ⇒ Riksbank hawkish ⇒ SEK rates up ⇒ you gain.

The borrowing rule is the one to keep. It replaces the whole formula: you locked your funding cheap, so you want that rate to rise. It works on every pair, every tenor, and never inverts — unlike the points direction, which flips depending on where USD sits.

Chain it: side → far leg → long/short points → which currency did I borrow → that rate up → borrowed USD or not → basis direction. Five seconds, no formula.

18 · Spreads — orders of magnitude

Indicative only. Calibrate these against your own screens — they move with volatility, size, time of day and who is axed.

Three rules that hold regardless of the numbers:

1. Wider with tenor, in pips. 2. Wider with illiquidity — Scandis and minors are multiples of the core pairs, not a few percent. 3. Client spreads are a multiple of interbank again.

But convert to bp and it inverts. Divide each spread by its own pips-per-bp (S × T) and the long end is tighter. A T/N spread of a fiftieth of a pip is worth tens of basis points annualised. The front end is the most expensive part of the curve in rate terms and the cheapest in cash.

19 · The front end — T/N, holidays, annualisation

What moves T/N. Not policy expectations — there is no meeting between tomorrow and the day after. It moves on liquidity and the short-dated basis: where overnight cash actually trades inside the corridor, and what one-day dollars cost. Central bank operations (Riksbank certificates, Norges Bank NOK purchases) hit exactly here.

Count the days, not the label. If T/N spans a weekend or a holiday in either currency it carries 3 days of interest, not 1 — so the points are ~3× normal. The screen still says "T/N". For anything under 1M, read the value dates, never the tenor.

The annualisation trap. 1 pip on T/N ≈ 30–40bp annualised, but only ~$800 on 100mio. Front-end implied yields swing wildly on trivial cash. Trust the cash, not the annualised rate — until the move persists, at which point term funding reprices with it.

Why the front end looks quiet. points ≈ S × Δr × t, and the t squashes short tenors. A 10bp move is 2.75 pips at 3M and 0.2 pips at 1W. Same repricing. Watch implied yields, not pips, inside 1M.

20 · Spot basis and off-market swaps

The spot rate used for the near leg is a free parameter. Sales can agree the points first and fix spot later, or deal at a spot away from market. Neither creates value — but both create a position.

spot delta of a matched swap = N × (1 − DFbase) ≈ N × rbase × T
fair points adjustment      = Δspot × rquote × T
value of moving spot basis   = N × (1 − DFquote) × Δspot

The delta does not depend on the spot basis. Near-leg spot and far-leg rate are contractual constants; only the base leg re-values with market spot. So "we are long the delta, let's buy it cheap by setting spot lower" is wrong — the delta is identical either way.

Re-price the points and you have made a loan. Dealing spot away from market moves cash today and reverses it at the far date with interest — at the quote currency rate. It is the same structure as a historic rate rollover: an embedded loan, with real credit and balance-sheet cost.

Leave the points unchanged and you have charged the client. That is where the apparent profit comes from: N × (1 − DFquote) × Δspot, visible to them the moment they mark it.

SensitivityFormulaDriven by
Swap's spot deltaN(1 − DFbase)base rate
Value of moving spot basisN(1 − DFquote) per unitquote rate

Worked example. 100mio GBP/USD 1Y buy/sell, GBP 4%, USD 3%, market 1.3500. Delta = +3.85mio GBP. Set spot to 1.3000 and re-price: points go from −129.8 to −144.8 (−15 pips = 0.05 × 3% × 1), and you have borrowed $5mio at 3% — you pay back $5.15mio. Set spot to 1.3000 and don't re-price: you take $145,631 from the client.

21 · NPV — what a swap is worth

NPV = the present value of every cash flow, converted into one currency at today's spot. A swap dealt at market is worth zero. That is the definition of "fair", not a statement that nothing is at risk.

NPV = PV(base legs) × Smkt + PV(quote legs)
for a matched swap: NPV ≈ N × Δpoints × DFquote
DF = 1 / (1 + r × T)

Zero NPV, non-zero legs. The base legs have PV = N(1 − DFbase) and the quote legs have the opposite value. They cancel in total but each is real — and that base-leg PV is your spot delta. Zero value, live risk.

Discount each currency on its own curve, then convert at spot (not at the forward). Using one curve for both destroys the PV mismatch and makes your system report a delta-neutral swap, which is wrong.

Reading the sign. Positive NPV = an asset: someone would pay you to take it over, you carry credit exposure to them, and under a CSA you receive collateral. Negative = the reverse. It is not a forecast of where the trade finishes.

Discounting is not noise: roughly 1% of the mark at 3M, ~8% at 2Y. Quote P&L undiscounted and you overstate every long-dated position.

22 · Borrow and lend — who pays what

Every FX swap is two simultaneous loans, secured against each other.

money IN at near, OUT at far = you BORROWED it
money OUT at near, IN at far = you LENT it

SwapBorrowedLentYou pay interest on
Buy / sellbasequotethe base
Sell / buyquotebasethe quote

The borrower always pays. In a swap you do both at once: you owe interest on what you borrowed and are owed interest on what you lent.

No interest is ever paid separately. The net of the two accruals is folded into the far-leg exchange rate — that is exactly what the forward points are. points ≈ S(rquote − rbase)t is not a market view, it is the arithmetic of netting two interest accruals into one rate.

It is a secured loan. Each side holds the other's currency for the whole period. That collateral is why swap-implied rates sit close to risk-free, and why the FX swap market keeps functioning when unsecured lending freezes.

The distinction that catches everyone: receiving the points ≠ being long the points. Buy/sell EUR/USD with positive points means you collect cash at the far leg (settlement) while being short the points (risk). One describes what settles, the other how your mark moves.

The three questions to ask of any position, in order: 1. Which way am I in the base currency on the far leg? 2. Is the currency that moved the base or the quote? 3. Am I the maker or the taker — and did I earn the spread or pay it?

Some desks name swaps by the far leg rather than the near leg. This trainer uses near-leg-first naming with the side defined by the far leg, which is the most common interbank convention. Rates used to generate points are plausible placeholders, not live market data.
